GitHub | Download

Platforms: Windows | Linux

Engine and Tools: C# | Monogame

About

One* Night at Miku's is a rogue-like action spin on classic Five Nights at Freddy's sit-and-survive gameplay. Study, learn, and master the machinations of the various nightly threats that stand between you and 6 AM.

Features

  • Fast-paced and addictive management gameplay where the most important resource is information, and the most costly action is hesitation.
  • 10+ gameplay modifiers (rogue-like style upgrades).
  • Compelling enemy AI.
  • Interesting(?) story.
  • 3 difficulty modes.
  • Lots and lots of easter eggs.
  • All the extras you'd expect of a FNaF style game (Custom night, etc.)

Development History

The idea for One* Night at Miku’s came similarly to that of Sha’s Tetris and Undertale Battle Engine. I was playing through the Five Nights at Frickbear’s games (a series of Five Nights at Freddy’s fan games) and thought, “Hey, I could probably make something like this!”

While Frickbear’s 3 is a well-renowned, polished, and expansive game, the first two in the series are scrappy Scratch games which reuse and reference many ideas. Having spent the previous two years of my life developing large-scale projects that now lie in varying states of immaterial, I was inspired by the games’ unabashed silliness and wanted to carry such an approach into my own work. Taking everything so seriously had caused me to never make anything at all. And here were two complete games that I had just played. Games that were fun, games that didn’t take themselves so seriously, games that were done! I decided I would attempt to rid myself of perfectionism by practicing “better is best.” This methodology is what guided my work on One* Night at Miku’s.

Design

In the spirit of low-stakes development, I made various scope cutting decisions early on:

  1. The majority of assets would be reused, specifically from various games in the FNaF series.
  2. The base gameplay loop will take heavy inspiration from that of FNaF 1.
  3. The game will aim to have high replayability in order to maximize playtime per development time.

    4. And most importantly,

  4. The codebase will be messy, hacky, and spaghetti-y. And that WILL be ok.

With ground rules in place, work began on planning out the game’s systems and mechanics. Continuing to take inspiration from Frickbear’s, I wanted to create a frantic and fast-paced gameplay loop where players feel like they’re constantly “patting their head and rubbing their belly.” It’s common for FNaF games to throw new things at the player each night, but I wanted a system that encouraged decision making. The actual nighttime gameplay wouldn’t change, but it would be progressively modified until it became controlled chaos.

Since the base night gameplay was fixed, I split development in half. I would first build the basic nightly gameplay loop and then build the modifiers on top. With the ability to focus solely on the night gameplay, I prototyped a recreation of the FNaF 1 office, along with its camera system.

As is tradition at this point, I used an image of a Miku plush as a placeholder to test the cameras.

I sent this prototype to a few friends to get their reactions and found it hilarious when they would tense up at the sight of a FNaF game, only to open the cameras and see Miku's silly little face staring back at them. This juxtaposition, when struck across my commitment to unseriousness, sparked what would become the core of the project in my mind.

However, while I was excited to stoke the flames of comedic juxtaposition, I was still deeply interested in making an engaging game. I wanted players to get in the door thinking, “what a joke, this is hilarious!” and leave, “I can’t believe I got hooked on such a goofy game. That was surprisingly compelling.” As such, I took the time to interrogate the design of FNaF-style games. Having grown up with the series and its fangames, I had my own opinions about what made a FNaF game “fun.” And to me, that was its moment to moment decision making.

With each game came a new set of rules to learn and threats to keep track of. The trial and error of figuring out how everything works feels almost playful in the way that the games purposefully drew your attention, guiding your discoveries. And then, once you'd come to understand the bounds of the game, the ways that your skills were tested by emergent moments of novel setups. Like a finding checkmate on an in-progress chess board. Finally besting the odds just to come back and do it again for another night, but this time there's even less room for error.

Those were the feelings I wanted to capture with my design, and I wanted to explore them through the lens of a much more action-oriented take on the genre. Much closer to FNaF 2 or UCN than FNaF 1 or 6.

And with that, we had…

One Night at Miku's

Implementing the features outlined in the design document was relatively straightforward. I first started with the three main Mikus.

Then I added the vents and Green.

And finally the Mikulings.

New to my workflow on this project, I regularly sent updated builds to friends as they were completed. This not only gave me a constant stream of feedback, but also helped to build forward momentum for the project as a whole. It also incentivized me to finalize implementations instead of leaving systems mostly-working which helped to avoid a lot of technical debt.

It was because of this feedback that I decided to include difficulty modes. From the start, One* Night at Miku's was developed with fans of not only FNaF, but FNaF fangames in mind. However, the one night nature of the game led to a learning curve too steep for players unfamiliar with FNaF conventions.

I was also able to refine the Mikus' ai in response to playtests, tuning for just the right game feel and flow. Some examples include:

  • Red and Yellow Miku originally said "Miku dayo" when at the doors, but this made it so you never had to check the office when in the cams.
  • Blue Miku would only say "seeeeggaaaa" when at the door and her call would be directional. This made her a one-note threat and so I made changes to keep her progress in the back of players' minds at all times.
  • Green Miku's movement was random. This meant he could enter the same vent over and over and over again as well as just wondering with no rhyme or reason. By making him pick a target, players are encouraged to study his movements more closely and try to predict his next move. Also, vent seals being a one-time solution felt more natural in an action context.

By this point I had spent some time working on the games menus and UI, leaving the overall package feeling relatively complete.

But it was still missing…

The Asterisk

With the base game in a somewhat complete state, I was able to shift my attention to developing the modifiers. For their design, I took inspiration from real life game modding. I wanted the changes they brought to feel coherent to the game as a whole, but just janky enough as to still be a little out of place. To this end, prioritizing the development of the base gameplay was a large boon. Not only did having a pre-existing framework to slot ideas in and out of help when devising new mods, but it also allowed their existence, down to the programming, to mimic that of a real game mod.

While each mod has a pro and a con, attributes were developed individually. This allowed me to explore an idea to its fullest, and then see where it fit in later. Developing this way also made it efficient to mix and match the effects of mods in attempts to find interesting and emergent combinations.

Naturally a few mods were left on the cutting room floor, but I am quite proud of the 11 in the game today.

With mods to make each night feel new, it was time to implement meta-progression.

Now all that’s left is to tie it all together! Should be easy, right? Well, you know what they say:

The Last Twenty Percent...

…is always 80% of the work. I would repeat this phrase over, and over, and over, and over again for the ensuing 4 months it took to release One* Night at Miku’s. Despite having a mostly complete game by this point, it was far from polished enough to be publicly facing. Modifiers needed to be balanced (both individually and as a whole), placeholder assets needed to be replaced, bugs needed to be fixed, menus needed to be polished, and all those cute little ideas I had for fun inclusions throughout development needed to be evaluated and either implemented or tossed.

This was a grueling process.

Not helped by the lack of motivation that comes with doing the game dev equivalent of paper work. Nor helped by the start of a very transitionary time in my life. Either way I made it through and I’m here to tell the tale. So here’s the few interesting bits:

I finally got around to making the tutorial! This was a white whale that loomed over me during most of the project. So much of what I enjoy about FNaF games is their sense of self-guided discovery and I struggled to preserve that while still ensuring first time players knew enough going in to have a fighting chance from the start. Initially I outlined a guided tour of the game which would be delivered by a character on the phone (as is tradition.) However, it felt too restrictive and honestly reminded me of forceful mobile game onboarding. Thinking maybe I wouldn’t reinvent the wheel for once, I then played around with having a classic nightly phone call that would explain the basics to players as they were already in the hot seat. While this did technically work, players’ attention was split between so many new things to learn that they struggled to retain much of use. Beyond this, the unchanging nature of nights in One* Night at Miku’s meant that the Mikus became active far too soon for any explanation to not feel rushed.

And that was where things stood for the majority of development. Just this looming dread that I’d have to figure it out sometime. Eventually, though, I settled on a mix of the two concepts. By having a self contained introduction to the game’s systems, players were able to learn the ropes without distraction. But by keeping specific information about each miku withheld, players still got to experiment on their own. Adding a few nightly calls also provided more forward movement to the game’s pacing, as well as maintaining the modifiers’ surprise introduction. This approach felt the most like teaching players how to fish, without doing it for them, or telling them just what kind of fish they might find…

For the phone character itself, I went through various drafts of its script. Finding the right balance of vague but informative was surprisingly difficult, and it wasn’t made any easier by my constantly changing character concepts. First it was an uninterested part-time worker, then a bumbling intern, then a well-meaning manager, then an asshole coworker, then the friend whose shift you’re covering, then,,, like,, Joel Last of Us???? I was all over the place and couldn’t land on one thing. Partly because I couldn’t get the voice right, but also because I (wanting to do the voice) couldn’t get the voice right. The final version is the result of me reaching my breaking point and falling back on the safe idea I had from the start. The tutorial voice being a little quirky with it came naturally because I’m just like that I guess.

With a tutorial finally in place, and a semblance of a narrative forming in the nightly calls, I realized I would probably need a more definitive ending than just a results screen. I’m not gonna explain the joke, I’m just gonna leave this here…

The final torment I faced in this phase of development was the Shadows. Both Office and Camera Shadow were originally modifier attributes, but they felt too substantial of a change to sit alongside the other mods. For a while they sat unused but as the game’s undertones grew darker, I thought it might be fun if they were semi-random encounters. Like entities outside of the game making their way in. At first there was no way to tell if a modifier was cursed, but in the spirit of interesting decision making based on ever-growing game knowledge, I made sure to add a way to tell. The hang up, then, was balancing their inclusion into the already stuffed gameplay loop.

The biggest struggle was deciding how present I wanted the shadows to be. I wanted them to be more like a haunting than a constant threat, and I liked how they could throw an unexpected wrench into players’ flow. The best example of this interplay was the late redesign of Camera Shadow. From first inclusion, it would appear on either cam 3 or 4 and then need to be stared at like in the final game. However, when it disappeared it would then move to another camera farther from the office, totaling three times the player needed to stare at it for it to finally go away. I enjoyed the sense of place that this mechanic gave to the location, but felt it reached outside of a supporting role.

At last One* Night at Miku’s was complete! It was finally time for…

Release

One* Night at Miku’s was the first independent game I gave a proper release and so I have some overall thoughts about the process.

When it comes to the game’s storepage, I pulled from conventional wisdom and did my best to include kinetic and varied screenshots, an eye-catching layout, and an enticing but easy to digest description. I centered messaging around the easy to understand (and hopefully compelling) phrase: “a rogue-like fnaf fangame.” This seemed to capture people’s attention, and gave a good introduction to what the game was about.

More unstructured, though, was the promotion of the game on Twitter and Youtube. I have no idea how to promote myself or my work, so I just put together a trailer and some clips that could be released periodically to hopefully attract attention. Begging for attention online feels gross so I limited the campaign to only a week before launch. I scheduled most uploads in advance and monitored the reaction. To my surprise a few people actually engaged with my posts!
