About

One* Night at Miku's is a rogue-like action spin on classic Five Nights at Freddy's sit-and-survive gameplay. Study, learn, and master the machinations of the various nightly threats that stand between you and 6 AM.

Features

  • Fast-paced and addictive management gameplay where the most important resource is information, and the most costly action is hesitation.
  • 10+ gameplay modifiers (rogue-like style upgrades).
  • Compelling enemy AI.
  • Interesting(?) story.
  • 3 difficulty modes.
  • Lots and lots of easter eggs.
  • All the extras you'd expect of a FNaF style game (Custom night, etc.)

Development History

The idea for One* Night at Miku's came similarly to that of Sha's Tetris and Undertale Battle Engine. I was playing through the Five Nights at Frickbear's games (a series of Five Nights at Freddy's fan games) and thought, "Hey, I could probably make something like this!"

While Frickbear's 3 is a well-renowned, polished, and expansive game, the first two in the series are scrappy Scratch games which reuse and reference many ideas. Having spent the previous two years of my life developing large-scale projects that now lie in varying states of immaterial, I was inspired by the games' unabashed silliness and wanted to carry such an approach into my own work. Taking everything so seriously had caused me to never make anything at all. And here were two complete games that I had just played. Games that were fun, games that didn't take themselves so seriously, games that were done! I decided I would attempt to rid myself of perfectionism by practicing “better is best.” This methodology is what guided my work on One* Night at Miku's.

Design

In the spirit of low-stakes development, I made various scope cutting decisions early on:

  1. The majority of assets would be reused, specifically from various games in the FNaF series.
  2. The base gameplay loop will take heavy inspiration from that of FNaF 1.
  3. The game will aim to have high replayability in order to maximize playtime per development time.

    4. And most importantly,

  4. The codebase will be messy, hacky, and spaghetti-y. And that WILL be ok.

With ground rules in place, work began on planning out the game's systems and mechanics. Continuing to take inspiration from Frickbear's, I wanted to create a frantic and fast-paced gameplay loop where players feel like they're “patting their head and rubbing their tummy.” This would be achieved by an ever-increasing number of roguelike style upgrades that complicate things as much as they helped.

In order to create such upgrades, a flexible (and slightly simplistic) base first had to be devised. To help with the pacing of development, I decided to split the work into two phases:

  1. Nighttime gameplay.
  2. Roguelike progression.

With the ability to focus solely on the night gameplay, I prototyped a recreation of the FNaF 1 office, along with its camera system.

As is tradition at this point, I used an image of a Miku plush as a placeholder to test the cameras.

I sent this prototype to a few friends to get their reactions and found it hilarious when they would tense up at the sight of a FNaF game, only to open the cameras and see Miku's silly little face staring back at them. This juxtaposition, when struck across my commitment to unseriousness, sparked what would become the core of the project in my mind.

However, while I was excited to stoke the flames of comedic juxtaposition, I was also deeply interested in making a fun game that would engage players. As such, I took the time to interrogate the design of FNaF-style games. Having grown up with the series and its fangames, I had my own opinions about what made a FNaF game "fun." And to me, that was its moment to moment decision making.

With each game came a new set of rules to learn and threats to keep track of. The trial and error of figuring out how everything works feels almost playful in the way that the games purposefully drew your attention, guiding your discoveries. And then, once you'd come to understand the bounds of the game, the ways that your skills were tested by emergent moments of novel setups. Like a finding checkmate on an in-progress chess board. Finally besting the odds just to come back and do it again for another night, but this time there's even less room for error.

Those were the feelings I wanted to capture with my design, and I wanted to explore them through the lens of a much more action-oriented take on the genre. Much closer to FNaF 2 or UCN than FNaF 1 or 6.

One Night at Miku's

Implementing the features outlined in the design document was relatively straightforward. I first started with the three main Mikus.

Then I added the vents and Green.

And finally the Mikulings.

New to my workflow on this project, I regularly sent updated builds to friends as they were completed. This not only gave me a constant stream of feedback, but also helped to build forward momentum for the project as a whole. It also incentivized me to finalize implementations instead of leaving systems mostly-working which helped to avoid a lot of technical debt.

It was because of this feedback that I decided to include difficulty modes. From the start, One* Night at Miku's was developed with fans of not only FNaF, but FNaF fangames in mind. However, the one night nature of the game led to a learning curve too steep for players unfamiliar with FNaF conventions.

I was also able to refine the Mikus' ai in response to playtests, tuning for just the right game feel and flow. Some examples include:

  • Red and Yellow Miku originally said "Miku dayo" when at the doors, but this made it so you never had to check the office when in the cams.
  • Blue Miku would only say "seeeeggaaaa" when at the door and her call would be directional. This made her a one-note threat and so I made changes to keep her progress in the back of players' minds at all times.
  • Green Miku's movement was random. This meant he could enter the same vent over and over and over again as well as just wondering with no rhyme or reason. By making him pick a target, players are encouraged to study his movements more closely and try to predict his next move. Also, vent seals being a one-time solution felt more natural in an action context.

By this point I had spent some time working on the games menus and UI, leaving the overall package feeling relatively complete.
